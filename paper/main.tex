\begin{abstract}
This study develops a coupled hourly simulation framework to quantify the competing thermal, humidity, soiling,
potential-induced degradation (PID) and chloride-corrosion effects on floating photovoltaic (FPV) systems in hot, humid
environments. A single over-water microclimate drives module temperature, module-surface humidity and dew, a dust and
sea-salt soiling balance with NaCl deliquescence, and a four-mechanism module degradation model. The mechanisms are
intrinsic ageing, Peck temperature--humidity damage with lagged encapsulant moisture, PID with salt-assisted surface
leakage, and corrosion scaled by time of wetness and chloride deposition. For a representative Visakhapatnam (India)
climate, the model compares coastal land PV, freshwater FPV and saline FPV. With the baseline parameters, the model
predicts year-1 yield differences relative to land of $+\GainYieldF$\% (freshwater) and $\GainYieldS$\% (saline), and
25-year energy differences of $+\GainLifeF$\% and $\GainLifeS$\%; the predicted saline degradation rate is
\DegTotS\%/yr against \DegTotL\%/yr on land. A \NMC-sample Monte Carlo over \NParams{} uncertain parameters, reported
separately from weather variability, gives median 25-year differences of $+$\MCLifeMedF\% (95\% interval
[\MCLifeLoF, \MCLifeHiF]\%) for freshwater FPV and \MCLifeMedS\% ([\MCLifeLoS, \MCLifeHiS]\%) for saline FPV. The model
therefore predicts a lifetime penalty for saline FPV across most of the plausible parameter space. The freshwater
benefit is conditional: it is positive in \MCLifePosF\% of samples, depends strongly on the thermal characteristics of the
floating structure, and vanishes if the dew point over the water rises by more than about \DTdStar~K. Mitigation is
modelled with explicitly assumed reduction factors, not inferred from certification. All results are model-based
scenario predictions that still require field validation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{F}{loating} photovoltaic (FPV) systems place PV arrays on pontoons over reservoirs, lakes, lagoons and
sheltered coastal waters. They avoid competition for land, reduce evaporation from water-supply reservoirs and can share
grid infrastructure with hydropower plants~\cite{worldbank2019,sahu2016,trapani2015,oliveirapinto2020,kumar2021}. India
has many reservoirs, salt pans and backwaters along a coast with high irradiation, so FPV is attractive there, but many
candidate sites are hot, humid and exposed to marine aerosol~\cite{makhija2023}.

The performance case for FPV usually rests on the cooling effect of the water. In tropical Singapore, well-ventilated
FPV designs ran cooler and reached higher performance ratios than a rooftop reference~\cite{liu2018}, with
irradiance-weighted temperatures up to \SI{14.5}{\celsius} lower~\cite{dorenkamper2021}. Offshore simulations also
predict higher yields than on land~\cite{golroodbari2020}. More recent measurements show that the cooling effect varies
widely with design. Modules that are not in thermal contact with the water behave much like land-based
modules~\cite{lindholm2021}. Measured heat-loss coefficients span \SIrange{22.6}{33.7}{\watt\per\square\metre\per\kelvin}
for several air-cooled systems~\cite{nysted2024,wu2024}, and wind-dependent terms of
\SIrange{2.7}{5.1}{\watt\second\per\cubic\metre\per\kelvin} have been reported~\cite{dorenkamper2024}. A multi-year,
humid-subtropical dataset found the FPV array only about \SI{0.8}{\kelvin} cooler than a co-located land
reference~\cite{firat2026}.

The water body also raises humidity and, at saline sites, airborne salt. The Peck model describes humidity-driven damage
as an Arrhenius function of temperature times a power of relative humidity~\cite{peck1986}, and moisture enters
polymer-backsheet modules over hours to days~\cite{kempe2006}. Combined-stressor lifetime models confirm that humidity
is a first-order variable~\cite{kaaya2019}. PID is strongly accelerated by humidity~\cite{pingel2010,hacke2011,
hoffmann2014,luo2017,bora2021}. Sea salt lowers the surface resistance of module glass by several orders of magnitude
as RH rises~\cite{hacke2015}, and salt raises leakage current~\cite{islam2018}. Field surveys report higher
degradation in hot climates~\cite{jordan2013}, and in India the hot--humid zones are among the worst
affected~\cite{dubey2014}. Chloride deposition and time of wetness define atmospheric corrosivity~\cite{iso9223}, and
salt mist is a module qualification stress~\cite{iec61701}. Salt crystallization dominates the deposits on coastal FPV
modules~\cite{ahmad2026}, and salt exposure acts on PV through soiling, corrosion, leakage-driven PID and barrier
ageing~\cite{goharian2026}.

Two recent studies come closest to this work. Mannino \emph{et al.}~\cite{mannino2023} applied existing degradation
models to onshore and offshore meteorological data. Ajmal \emph{et al.}~\cite{ajmal2025} added salinity to climatic
degradation models for offshore modules and calibrated them by optimization. Table~\ref{tab:lit} compares the scope of
representative studies. Among the studies reviewed here, none was identified that resolves, within one consistent
microclimate, both the yield benefits of FPV and the humidity-, salt- and PID-driven penalties, and that compares
freshwater with saline sites over the plant lifetime and in cost terms.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A coupled hourly model chain in which one over-water microclimate drives module temperature, dew, spectral
response, soiling and four module-degradation mechanisms, so that their trade-offs are resolved consistently.
\item A moisture formulation based on module-surface RH with a lagged encapsulant response. It captures why cooler
modules can see more moisture, and it couples PID to salt-assisted surface leakage.
\item A comparison of coastal land PV, freshwater FPV and saline FPV covering year-1 losses, mechanism-resolved
degradation, 25-year energy and LCOE. Mitigation options are evaluated one by one, with explicitly assumed effects.
\item An uncertainty analysis that separates weather variability (\NSeeds{} synthetic years) from parameter
uncertainty (a \NMC-sample Monte Carlo over \NParams{} parameters). It is complemented by break-even thresholds for
salt deposition, chloride deposition and dew-point rise, and by economic break-even values for CAPEX, land cost and
water value.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Scope.} The degradation results describe the \emph{electrical degradation of the PV modules}. Degradation of the
floating system itself (fatigue of floats and moorings, corrosion of structures~\cite{xiong2025,feng2025}, storm
damage, bio-fouling) is outside the model, so the lifetime energy reported here is not a complete FPV lifetime
prediction.

\subsection{Weather and Over-Water Microclimate}\label{sec:weather}
The synthetic year draws daily rain occurrence and amounts, a first-order autoregressive daily clearness index and
daily temperature anomalies from monthly normals. Hourly GHI is the Haurwitz clear-sky value~\cite{haurwitz1945}
scaled by the clearness index. Air temperature follows an asymmetric diurnal cycle with its minimum near 05:00 and its
maximum near 14:00. The dew point $T_d$ is held constant over each day, which reproduces the diurnal swing of RH, and
is shifted per month so that the monthly mean RH matches the normal. Wind speed includes a mean-preserving afternoon
sea breeze. Table~\ref{tab:weather} compares \NSeeds{} generated years with the normals they are built from. This shows
that the generator is climatologically consistent with its inputs; it is \emph{not} an external validation, because no
measured hourly series was available to this study. The RH match is exact by construction, and the monthly
clearness index (hence GHI) is a calibrated input that has not been checked against measurements.

Over water, the air temperature is pulled towards the water temperature $T_w$, taken as the 30-day running mean of air
temperature plus \SI{0.5}{\kelvin}. The dew point rises by $\Delta T_d$ because of evaporation, and the wind speed
increases over the smoother surface:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
T_a' = T_a + \alpha\,(T_w - T_a),\qquad v' = k_v v,\\
T_d' = \min(T_d + \Delta T_d,\,T_a').
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:micro}
\end{equation}
Relative humidity follows from the Magnus relation $e_s(T)=6.112\exp[17.62T/(243.12+T)]$~hPa as
$\mathit{RH}=100\,e_s(T_d')/e_s(T_a')$.

\subsection{Irradiance, Incidence and Spectrum}
Solar position uses Spencer's series~\cite{spencer1971}. GHI is decomposed with the Erbs correlation~\cite{erbs1982}
and transposed to the array plane with the Hay--Davies model~\cite{hay1980}:
\begin{multline}
G = G_b\cos\theta + G_d\left[A_i R_b + (1-A_i)\tfrac{1+\cos\beta}{2}\right]\\
+ \rho\,G_h\tfrac{1-\cos\beta}{2},
\label{eq:poa}
\end{multline}
where $\theta$ is the angle of incidence, $\beta$ the tilt, $A_i$ the anisotropy index, $R_b$ the beam ratio and $\rho$
the albedo (0.07 for water, 0.20 for land). Beam reflection losses use the ASHRAE modifier
$\mathrm{IAM}=1-b_0(1/\cos\theta-1)$ with $b_0=0.05$; diffuse and ground-reflected light take a constant factor of 0.95.
Humid air absorbs mainly in infrared bands where c-Si responds weakly, so the mono-Si spectral factor is computed from
the Kasten--Young air mass $\mathit{AM}$~\cite{kasten1989} and the precipitable water $w$~\cite{gueymard1994}:
\begin{equation}
M = c_1 + c_2\mathit{AM} + c_3 w + c_4\sqrt{\mathit{AM}} + c_5\sqrt{w} + c_6\,\mathit{AM}/\sqrt{w},
\label{eq:spectral}
\end{equation}
with the coefficients of~\cite{lee2016}.

\subsection{Module Temperature, Surface Humidity and Dew}
Module temperature follows the Faiman model~\cite{faiman2008}. A net long-wave loss of
$q_{\mathrm{sky}}=\SI{40}{\watt\per\square\metre}$ to the night sky applies when $G<\SI{50}{\watt\per\square\metre}$:
\begin{equation}
T_m = T_a' + \frac{G - q_{\mathrm{sky}}\,\mathbb{1}[G<50]}{U_0 + U_1 v'}.
\label{eq:faiman}
\end{equation}
The land coefficients are the open-rack defaults. The baseline FPV coefficients (Table~\ref{tab:params}) represent a
well-ventilated pontoon design~\cite{liu2018,dorenkamper2021}. Because measured values for other designs are
considerably lower~\cite{nysted2024,wu2024,dorenkamper2024,firat2026}, the Monte Carlo ranges include FPV coefficients
no better than those of open racks. The RH of the air layer in contact with the glass is
\begin{equation}
\RHs = 100\,e_s(T_d')/e_s(T_m).
\label{eq:rhs}
\end{equation}
Dew forms when $T_m - T_d' < \SI{1}{\kelvin}$ and persists after sunrise until $T_m - T_d' > \SI{4}{\kelvin}$. While dew
is present, an optical loss of 4\% is applied. This value is an engineering assumption, not a measured quantity;
Section~\ref{sec:dew} shows that the conclusions are insensitive to it over 0--6\%.

\subsection{Dust and Sea-Salt Soiling}
Dust and salt surface densities evolve by a daily mass balance. Dust accumulates at $r_d$, and salt at $r_s\,v/v_0$
with $v_0=\SI{3}{\metre\per\second}$, because sea-spray generation rises with wind. Rain above \SI{2}{mm/day} removes a
fraction $\eta_d f_\beta$ of the dust and $\eta_s f_\beta$ of the salt, with $\eta_d = 0.80$, $\eta_s = 0.95$ (salt is
soluble) and $f_\beta=\sin\beta/\sin\ang{20}$. When the daily maximum RH exceeds the NaCl deliquescence point of 75\%,
dissolved salt recrystallizes as a crust that cements dust to the glass~\cite{ahmad2026}; on such days the
dust-cleaning efficiency is reduced by the cementation factor (baseline 0.5). All plants are cleaned every 15~days with
95\% efficiency. The soiling ratio is
\begin{equation}
\mathit{SR} = \max\left(\mathit{SR}_{\min},\,1 - k_d m_d - k_s m_s\right),
\label{eq:soiling}
\end{equation}
with $k_d=0.035$ and $k_s=\SI{0.060}{\square\metre\per\gram}$. Two salt quantities are distinguished throughout.
The \emph{salt deposition} $r_s$ (\si{\gram\per\square\metre\per\day}) is the mass of sea salt, treated as NaCl,
retained on the module glass, which drives soiling and surface leakage. The \emph{chloride deposition} $D_{\mathrm{Cl}}$
(\si{\milli\gram\per\square\metre\per\day}) is the chloride-ion deposition rate of the atmosphere in the ISO~9223
sense~\cite{iso9223}, which drives corrosion. The deposition rates and coefficients are not site-measured, so they are
varied in Sections~\ref{sec:thresholds} and~\ref{sec:uq}.

\subsection{Electrical Model}
The effective irradiance $G_{\mathrm{eff}}$ includes the incidence, spectral, soiling and dew corrections. DC power
follows the PVWatts model~\cite{dobos2014}:
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{dc}} = P_0\,\frac{G_{\mathrm{eff}}}{1000}\left[1+\gamma(T_m-25)\right]\prod_j(1-\ell_j),
\label{eq:pdc}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma=\SI{-0.0035}{\per\kelvin}$ and $\ell_j$ are the wiring, mismatch, wave-motion and light-induced
degradation (LID, 1\%) losses. The PVWatts inverter curve (98\% nominal) with clipping and an availability factor
follow.

\subsection{Module Degradation Model}
The annual module degradation rate is the sum of four mechanisms:
\begin{equation}
R = R_0 + R_{\mathrm{TH}} + R_{\mathrm{PID}} + R_{\mathrm{C}},
\label{eq:R}
\end{equation}
where $R_0=0.30$\%/yr covers ultraviolet exposure and thermal cycling. Encapsulant moisture follows the surface
humidity with a first-order lag ($\tau=\SI{48}{h}$)~\cite{kempe2006}:
\begin{equation}
\RHe(t) = \RHe(t-1) + \kappa\,\bigl[\RHs(t)-\RHe(t-1)\bigr],
\label{eq:rhe}
\end{equation}
with $\kappa = 1-e^{-\Delta t/\tau}$. With the Arrhenius factor
\begin{equation}
a_E(T_m) = \exp\!\left[-\frac{E}{k_B}\left(\frac{1}{T_m}-\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)\right],
\label{eq:arr}
\end{equation}
the Peck rate~\cite{peck1986} averaged over the year is
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{TH}} = R_{\mathrm{TH}}^{\mathrm{ref}}\left\langle a_{E_a}(T_m)
\left(\frac{\phi\,\RHe}{\mathit{RH}_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^{n}\right\rangle,
\label{eq:peck}
\end{equation}
with $E_a=\SI{0.79}{eV}$, $n=3$, $T_{\mathrm{ref}}=\SI{25}{\celsius}$ and $\mathit{RH}_{\mathrm{ref}}=60\%$. The factor
$\phi=1$ applies to standard glass--backsheet modules. Because $\RHs$ depends on $T_m$, cooling a module lowers the
Arrhenius factor but raises $\RHe$; \eqref{eq:peck} resolves this trade-off.

PID acts only while the array is energized ($G>\SI{50}{\watt\per\square\metre}$):
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{PID}} = R_{\mathrm{PID}}^{\mathrm{ref}}(1-\mu_{\mathrm{PID}})
\bigl\langle a_{E_{\mathrm{PID}}}(T_m)\,f_{\mathrm{leak}}\bigr\rangle_{\mathrm{on}},
\label{eq:pid}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
f_{\mathrm{leak}} = \left[1+\exp\left(-\frac{\mathit{RH} - (\mathit{RH}_{50} - \Delta_s\,s)}{5}\right)\right]^{-1},
\label{eq:leak}
\end{equation}
where $E_{\mathrm{PID}}=\SI{0.9}{eV}$, $\mathit{RH}_{50}=70\%$ and $s=\min(m_s/0.5,1)$ is the salt coverage. Two levels
of support should be distinguished. \emph{Experimentally supported physics:} PID leakage rises steeply with
humidity~\cite{hoffmann2014,luo2017}, and sea salt lowers the surface resistance of module glass by orders of magnitude
as RH rises and increases leakage current~\cite{hacke2015,islam2018}. \emph{Assumed model representation:} the
logistic form of \eqref{eq:leak}, its 5\%-RH width, $\mathit{RH}_{50}$ and the threshold shift $\Delta_s=15\%$~RH for
a full salt film are modelling choices that have not been fitted to data.

The corrosion rate follows the ISO~9223 logic of time of wetness and chloride deposition~\cite{iso9223}:
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{C}} = R_{\mathrm{C}}^{\mathrm{ref}}\,\frac{\TOWsym}{\TOWsym_{\mathrm{ref}}}
\sqrt{\frac{D_{\mathrm{Cl}}}{D_{\mathrm{ref}}}}\,(1-\mu_{\mathrm{C}}),
\label{eq:corr}
\end{equation}
where $\TOWsym$ counts the hours with $\mathit{RH}>80\%$ or dew on the module, $\TOWsym_{\mathrm{ref}}=\SI{2500}{h}$ and
$D_{\mathrm{ref}}=\SI{60}{\milli\gram\per\square\metre\per\day}$.

The reference rates ($R_{\mathrm{TH}}^{\mathrm{ref}}=0.10$, $R_{\mathrm{PID}}^{\mathrm{ref}}=0.06$ and
$R_{\mathrm{C}}^{\mathrm{ref}}=0.10$\%/yr) were calibrated so that standard modules on land at this site degrade by
roughly 0.9--1\%/yr. That is the order of magnitude reported for hot--humid Indian climates~\cite{dubey2014}, above
the global median of about 0.5\%/yr~\cite{jordan2013}. They are calibration constants, not measured site values, and
are varied over a factor of about two in the Monte Carlo.

\subsection{Mitigation Options and Their Assumed Effects}\label{sec:mit}
Qualification standards define test procedures; they do not establish a numerical reduction of field degradation. The
mitigation cases are therefore described by the technology and the standard used to qualify it, and their effect
enters the model through \emph{explicitly assumed} factors (Table~\ref{tab:mitstd}). Salt-mist resistance is qualified
with IEC~61701~\cite{iec61701}. PID susceptibility of crystalline-silicon modules is evaluated with IEC~TS~62804-1
\cite{iec62804}; accelerated PID tests have been used to predict field PID rates~\cite{li2025}. The factors
$\mu_{\mathrm{PID}}$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{C}}$ are sampled over wide ranges in Section~\ref{sec:uq}. In the rest of the
paper, ``(M)'' denotes modules with both PID-resistant technology and salt-mist hardening.

\subsection{Lifetime Energy and Economics}
Year-$y$ energy is $E_y = E_1(1-R/100)^{y-1}$ over $N=25$ years. The lifetime projection assumes a constant effective
annual degradation rate derived from the stress distribution of the simulated year; the 25-year results therefore
represent an equivalent-rate scenario, not a mechanistic year-by-year degradation trajectory. The LCOE is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{LCOE} = \frac{C_0 + \sum_{y=1}^{N} O_y(1+r)^{-y} - \sum_{y=1}^{N} B_y(1+r)^{-y}}{\sum_{y=1}^{N}E_y(1+r)^{-y}},
\label{eq:lcoe}
\end{equation}
with a discount rate $r=9\%$ and O\&M costs $O_y$ escalating by 5\%/yr. $B_y = c_w V_w$ is an optional water benefit:
for the freshwater plant, $V_w$ is the evaporation avoided by shading (\SI{10}{\square\metre\per\kilo\watt p}, 65\%
suppression of \SI{1800}{mm/yr} pan evaporation) and $c_w$ a water value. The costs are illustrative Indian-market
values, so the economic conclusions are location- and year-dependent. Because LCOE is linear in $C_0$ and $c_w$,
break-even values follow in closed form.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification}\label{sec:uq}
Two sources of uncertainty are treated separately. \emph{Weather variability} is represented by \NSeeds{} independent
synthetic years. \emph{Parameter uncertainty} is propagated by Monte Carlo sampling of \NParams{} parameters
(\NMC{} samples): the three degradation reference rates, the dew optical loss (0--6\%), the soiling coefficients and
cementation factor, the land and FPV heat-loss coefficients ($U_0$: 22--29 and \SIrange{22}{45}{\watt\per\square\metre
\per\kelvin}; $U_1$: 4.5--8 and \SIrange{2.5}{10}{\watt\second\per\cubic\metre\per\kelvin}), the wind factor, the
wave-motion mismatch, $\alpha$ and $\Delta T_d$ for each FPV site, the dust, salt and chloride deposition
(log-uniform, $0.5\times$--$2\times$), $\mu_{\mathrm{PID}}$, $\mu_{\mathrm{C}}$, the CAPEX of each plant, the discount
rate (6--12\%) and the O\&M escalation (2--7\%/yr). All are sampled independently from uniform or log-uniform
distributions; Table~\ref{tab:params_mc} in the Appendix lists every parameter with its baseline value, distribution,
bounds and justification. Parameters
shared by the plants (e.g., the reference rates) take the same draw in all scenarios of a sample, so the scenario
differences are paired. Spearman rank correlations identify the dominant parameters. The ranges, seeds and baseline
values are stored in a machine-readable file in the repository.

\subsection{Module Degradation}
Table~\ref{tab:deg} and Fig.~\ref{fig:deg} resolve the modelled degradation by mechanism. Floating modules are cooler,
but their encapsulant equilibrates at a higher moisture level: mean $\RHe$ is \RHencL\%, \RHencF\% and \RHencS\% at the
land, freshwater and saline plants. Through the cubic humidity term of \eqref{eq:peck}, this cancels the Arrhenius
benefit of cooling. $R_{\mathrm{TH}}$ is \DegTHL, \DegTHF{} and \DegTHS\%/yr. In this model, therefore, \emph{cooling by
water does not translate into slower moisture-driven ageing at a humid site}. PID is predicted at \DegPIDL, \DegPIDF{}
and \DegPIDS\%/yr; the saline value is highest because the salt film makes the glass conductive at lower RH. Corrosion
separates the sites most clearly. Time of wetness rises from \TOWL~h/yr on land to \TOWF{} and \TOWS~h/yr over water,
and with \SI{180}{\milli\gram\per\square\metre\per\day} of chloride, $R_{\mathrm{C}}$ reaches \DegCorrS\%/yr at the
saline plant (\DegCorrL\%/yr on land). Under the baseline assumptions, total degradation is \DegTotL, \DegTotF{} and
\DegTotS\%/yr.

\subsection{Thresholds for Salinity and Humidity}\label{sec:thresholds}
Fig.~\ref{fig:thr}(a) varies the salt-soiling deposition of the saline plant with the chloride load held at baseline.

\subsection{Economics and Break-Even Values}
Under the assumed costs, FPV is more expensive per kWh: \LCOEF~INR/kWh (freshwater) and \LCOES~INR/kWh (saline) against
\LCOEL~INR/kWh on land. In the Monte Carlo, freshwater FPV is more expensive than land PV in \PLcoeF\% of samples
and saline FPV in \PLcoeS\%. Solving \eqref{eq:lcoe} for parity
with land PV gives the following break-even values:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{CAPEX}: \CapexStarF~INR/Wp for freshwater FPV (baseline 45) and \CapexStarS~INR/Wp for saline FPV
(baseline 52).
\item \emph{Land cost}: land PV would need a further \LandPremF~INR/Wp of land-related cost to match freshwater FPV,
and \LandPremS~INR/Wp to match saline FPV.
\item \emph{Water value}: valuing the avoided evaporation (\WaterSaved~\si{\cubic\metre} per MWp and year) lowers the
freshwater LCOE to \LCOEWaterTen, \LCOEWaterTwenty{} and \LCOEWaterForty~INR/kWh at 10, 20 and
40~INR/\si{\cubic\metre}. Parity needs about \WaterStar~INR/\si{\cubic\metre}.
\end{itemize}
The financing assumptions shift all LCOEs but not their ranking (Table~\ref{tab:fin}). Over a discount rate of 6--12\%
and an O\&M escalation of 2--7\%/yr, the LCOE gap to land PV ranges from \FinGapFMin{} to \FinGapFMax~INR/kWh for
freshwater FPV and from \FinGapSMin{} to \FinGapSMax~INR/kWh for saline FPV; both parameters are also sampled in the
Monte Carlo. FPV is thus justified mainly where land is scarce or costly, where reservoir water has a high value, or
where FPV CAPEX falls substantially. No salinity level makes saline FPV cost-competitive at the assumed CAPEX. Because
CAPEX, financing, O\&M, land and water values vary between locations and over time, these economic results are
specific to the stated Indian-market assumptions.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Comparison With Previous Work}
The baseline thermal advantage of FPV (about 3~K in daytime, 7~K at peak) lies within the range reported for open,
well-ventilated designs~\cite{liu2018,dorenkamper2021}. It exceeds the advantage measured for less ventilated
systems~\cite{nysted2024,firat2026}. The Monte Carlo makes this explicit: with FPV heat-loss coefficients at the low end
of the measured range, the freshwater FPV advantage disappears. The predicted salt-driven increase in degradation is
larger than the 0.05--0.13 percentage-point effect of salt reported for offshore modules~\cite{ajmal2025}. The
difference arises because this work also includes salt soiling, salt-assisted PID and chloride corrosion, whose
calibration remains uncertain. Mannino \emph{et al.}~\cite{mannino2023} found that degradation models need coefficients
calibrated to the installation environment; that conclusion applies equally to the reference rates used here.

\subsection{Decision-Relevant Drivers}
The rank correlations of Fig.~\ref{fig:unc}(b) show which inputs decide the comparison, and therefore which should be
measured first at a candidate site. For freshwater FPV these are the heat-loss coefficients of the floating structure and
the dust load at the land alternative. For saline FPV they are the same thermal and dust inputs together with the salt
deposition on the glass and the chloride deposition of the atmosphere. Together with the
thresholds of Section~\ref{sec:thresholds}, this gives two practical rules. First, the lifetime benefit of freshwater FPV
depends strongly on the thermal characteristics of the floating structure; it should not be assumed from the presence of
water alone. Second, the dew-point (humidity) increment over the water should be measured before a lifetime benefit is
claimed, because the benefit vanishes above about \DTdStar~K.

\subsection{Implications for Design and Siting}
The analysis separates two questions that are often merged: whether FPV produces more energy in year 1, and whether it
produces more over its life. For freshwater reservoirs, the baseline answer is yes to both, but the answer depends
mainly on the float design's heat-loss coefficients and on the humidity increment over the water. For saline sites,
both baseline answers are no, and the lifetime deficit holds over most of the parameter space. These model-based
findings suggest the following:
\begin{itemize}
\item Verify the heat-loss coefficients of the chosen float design and measure RH and dew point over the water before
relying on a lifetime gain.
\item At saline or near-shore sites, select PID-resistant module technology (qualified with IEC~TS~62804-1) and modules
qualified for salt mist (IEC~61701). Consider low-permeability glass--glass packages, and treat the size of their field
benefit as uncertain until field data exist.
\item Use negative-pole grounding or night-time PID recovery, marine-grade connectors and corrosion-resistant
structures designed for the ISO~9223 corrosivity class of the site.
\item At saline sites, shorten the cleaning interval in the dry, windy pre-monsoon season, using demineralized water.
\item Monitor module temperature, RH at the array, insulation resistance and soiling in line with
IEC~61724-1~\cite{iec61724}, so that the parameters can be recalibrated with field data.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Validation.} The weather is a synthetic year that reproduces its input normals (Table~\ref{tab:weather})
but has not been checked against a measured hourly series. No FPV module-temperature or performance data were
available to validate the thermal, soiling or degradation models.
\item \emph{Assumed parameters.} The over-water microclimate, the deposition rates, the degradation reference rates and
the mitigation factors are assumptions. Their uncertainty is propagated, but the ranges themselves are judgements.
\item \emph{Degradation physics.} The model is linear in time and additive across mechanisms. It does not represent PID
saturation or recovery, interactions between mechanisms, abrupt failures of corroded interconnects, or the higher
operating temperature of glass--glass modules.
\item \emph{System scope.} Float and mooring fatigue, structural corrosion~\cite{xiong2025,feng2025}, storm damage,
bird soiling and bio-fouling are not modeled.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
A coupled thermal, soiling and module-degradation model was used to compare land-mounted, freshwater floating and
saline floating PV for a hot, humid, coastal site in India. With the baseline parameters, the model predicts that water
cooling lowers daytime module temperature by \dTdayF~K and gives freshwater FPV $+\GainYieldF$\% year-1 yield and
$+\GainLifeF$\% 25-year energy relative to land. The same water body raises the humidity at the modules, and in the
model this cancels the expected temperature benefit for moisture-driven degradation. At the saline site, salt soiling,
salt-assisted PID and chloride corrosion raise the predicted degradation rate to \DegTotS\%/yr, and the baseline
25-year energy falls by $\GainLifeS$\%.

Parameter uncertainty is much larger than weather variability, and it changes the interpretation. The Monte Carlo
median 25-year difference is $+$\MCLifeMedF\% for freshwater FPV, with a 95\% interval of [\MCLifeLoF, \MCLifeHiF]\%. The
freshwater benefit is therefore conditional: it depends strongly on the thermal characteristics of the floating structure
and on the humidity increment over the water, and it vanishes above a dew-point rise of about \DTdStar~K. For saline FPV
the median is \MCLifeMedS\% ([\MCLifeLoS, \MCLifeHiS]\%), so the model predicts a lifetime penalty across most of the
plausible parameter space. Under the baseline parameterization, salinity is the dominant driver of that penalty. Module
selection for PID and salt-mist resistance is the most effective modelled mitigation, but its size rests on assumed
reduction factors.

These are model-based scenario predictions that still require field validation, for which a measurement protocol is
proposed. Assessments of FPV in humid and saline regions should measure the thermal characteristics of the floating
structure and the humidity increment over the water, and should report lifetime energy with mechanism-resolved
degradation and quantified uncertainty, not first-year yield alone.
